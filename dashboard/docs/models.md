# Mathematical Models Reference

This document describes the mathematical models used in the dashboard, their parameters, and their limitations. Models referenced in the paper UPDATE v.A-29 are listed in Section 2.

---

## 1. Super-exponential Resistance Curve (Gratacós-Botto)

### Formula

The primary resistance trajectory plotted on the Overview page is a generalised logistic with a time-quadratic term in the exponent — the closed-form expression of the paper's "rate of increase is itself growing" thesis (UPDATE v.A-29 párr. 22):

```
y(τ) = K / (1 + A · exp(-r·τ - b·τ²))     where τ = year - 1990
```

The crucial term is `-b·τ²`. Its derivative with respect to time gives an effective rate

```
r_eff(τ) = r + 2·b·τ
```

that grows linearly with τ — exactly the "super-exponential" behaviour the paper describes. With `b = 0` the formula reduces to a standard constant-rate logistic.

### Parameters (hardcoded, no fitting)

| Parameter | Value     | Meaning |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| `K`       | 100       | Carrying capacity — theoretical near-total ineffectiveness. |
| `A`       | 88/12 ≈ 7.3333 | Determined analytically by `y(1990) = 12`. |
| `r`       | 0.0705    | Base growth rate. |
| `b`       | 3.05·10⁻⁴ | Super-exponential acceleration. `r_eff` grows by ≈ 2b·τ per year of elapsed τ. |
| `t0`      | 1990      | Time origin. |

These coefficients were calibrated by hand to the paper's milestones (párr. 19): `y(2025) = 70`, `y(2032) ≈ 80`, `y(2040) ≈ 90`, `y(2047) ≈ 96`, `y(2060) ≈ 99`. The model is **fully deterministic** — no random sampling, no stochastic optimisation, identical output on every reload.

### Reference logistic (for comparison)

The dashboard also plots a faint dotted reference curve corresponding to the same `K`, `A`, `t0` but with `b = 0`. Its rate `r' ≈ 0.0811` is calibrated so the reference also passes through `y(1990) = 12` and `y(2025) = 70`. The reference curve crosses the critical threshold (95) in **2051**; the super-exponential crosses it in **2047** — a 4-year advance, which is the central forecasting claim of the paper.

### Uncertainty band (±3 years)

The shaded blue envelope around the primary curve is generated by **laterally shifting** the trajectory by ±3 years (paper párr. 5: "fourteen years (±3)"). It is a deterministic visualisation of temporal uncertainty, not a statistical confidence interval.

### Implementation

`dashboard/data/amr_data.py` exposes:

- `compute_super_exponential_curve(start, end)` — primary model
- `compute_reference_logistic_curve(start, end)` — constant-r reference
- `compute_sigmoid_curve(start, end)` — legacy piecewise interpolation between hardcoded anchor points (kept for backward compatibility)

### Interpretation

The Resistance Pressure Index is a **conceptual composite metric** on a 0–100 scale:

- 0 — no clinically significant resistance (pre-antibiotic baseline).
- ~70 (current, 2025) — widespread resistance to multiple first-line agents; carbapenems increasingly compromised.
- ~95 (critical threshold) — near-total ineffectiveness of first-line antibiotics for hospital-acquired Gram-negative infections.
- 100 — theoretical complete pan-resistance (asymptote, never fully reached).

This index does not correspond to any single measured quantity. It synthesises trends in MRSA prevalence, ESBL/3GC-R rates, carbapenem resistance, and other indicators into a single trajectory for communication purposes.

## 2. Mathematical Model Families in the AMR Literature

The paper's Math section catalogues a number of model families that have been applied to antimicrobial-resistance dynamics. These are not implemented in the dashboard but are listed here for methodological context — they could underpin future quantitative extensions (e.g. the antimetabolic page).

### 2.1 Within-host / between-host integrated models

Population-level dynamics that explicitly couple within-host bacterial population dynamics (clonal expansion, mutation) with between-host transmission.

- **Niewiadomska et al.** "Population-level mathematical modeling of antimicrobial resistance." *Infect. Dis. Model.*, 2019.
- **Temime, L. et al.** "The rising impact of mathematical modelling in epidemiology: a case study of antibiotic resistance." *J. R. Soc. Interface*, 2007.

### 2.2 Multiscale models (mutation + selection + horizontal gene transfer)

Track resistance gene movement between cells in addition to clonal selection — important because plasmid-mediated transfer (KPC, NDM, OXA-48) is a major driver of carbapenem resistance.

- "A multiscale model of antibiotic resistance considering mutation, selection, and gene transfer." *Proc. R. Soc. B*, 2019.
- "A model of resistance evolution in the presence of horizontal gene transfer." *Theor. Popul. Biol.*, 2015.
- "A model of resistance-gene persistence in the absence of antibiotic use." *PLoS Comput. Biol.*, 2017.

### 2.3 Mechanistic cycling/mixing and combination-therapy models

Compare antibiotic-cycling vs antibiotic-mixing vs combination-therapy strategies on the trajectory of resistance.

- **Pimenta, M. L. et al.** "A mechanistic model for antibiotic-resistance development under antibiotic cycling and mixing." *Microb. Drug Resist.*, 2020.
- "A model-based evaluation of antibiotic cycling and mixing on resistance evolution." *Microb. Drug Resist.*, 2017.
- "A model of antibiotic resistance in the context of combination therapy and rotating regimens." *Front. Microbiol.*, 2018.
- **Michel, J. B. et al.** "Differential impact of antibiotic interactions on the evolution of resistance." *Nature*, 2008.
- **Hegreness, M. et al.** "Antagonistic drug interactions can stimulate the evolution of resistance." *PNAS*, 2008.
- **Nashebi, R. et al.** "Mathematical modelling of antibiotic interaction on evolution of antibiotic resistance: an analytical approach." *PeerJ*, 2024.

### 2.4 Pharmacodynamic models (sub-therapeutic dosing & intermittent exposure)

Concentration-dependent killing, post-antibiotic effects, and sub-therapeutic windows that drive (or avoid) resistance emergence.

- "A pharmacodynamic model of antibiotic resistance development under sub-therapeutic dosing." *J. Theor. Biol.*, 2019.
- "A model of resistance-evolution under intermittent antibiotic exposure." *J. R. Soc. Interface*, 2016.

### 2.5 Hospital-network / transmission models

Healthcare-system contact networks bound the population-scale impact of any therapeutic strategy. Includes specific CRE colonisation models.

- "A network model of antibiotic resistance spread in healthcare systems." *Sci. Rep.*, 2020.
- "A stochastic model of antibiotic resistance transmission in a hospital ward." *Math. Biosci.*, 2018.
- "A mathematical model of antibiotic treatment in hospital-acquired MRSA infection." *J. Theor. Biol.*, 2015.
- **Ong, K. M. et al.** "A mathematical model and inference method for bacterial colonization and transmission of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae." *PLoS One*, 2020.
- "A Model-Based Strategy to Control the Spread of Carbapenem-Resistant Enterobacteriaceae: Simulate and Implement." *Clin. Infect. Dis.*, 2016.
- "A model of resistance-evolution under antibiotic pressure in hospital vs community settings." *(paper UPDATE v.A-29 ref 108)*

### 2.6 Biofilm models

Biofilm-bound bacteria respond differently to therapy — relevant for chronic and indwelling-device infections (P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, S. maltophilia).

- "A model for antibiotic resistance in biofilms under continuous antibiotic exposure." *Bull. Math. Biol.*, 2020.

### 2.7 Stewardship and modelling of policy interventions

Models of stewardship-program outcomes and policy levers.

- "Mathematical modeling of antimicrobial resistance in the context of antibiotic stewardship programs." *Bull. Math. Biol.*, 2021.

### 2.8 Gut microbiome and repeated-exposure models

Dynamics of resistance gene reservoirs under repeated antibiotic courses.

- "A model for antimicrobial resistance in the gut microbiota under repeated antibiotic exposure." *Microbiome*, 2020.

### 2.9 Time-series forecasting (SARIMA / ARIMA)

Used in the dashboard's Time Series page (see Section 3 below).

- **Kim, J. et al.** "Predicting antimicrobial resistance of bacterial pathogens using time-series data from >600 farms." *Front. Microbiol.*, 2023.
- "Time series analysis of antibacterial usage and bacterial resistance in China: observations from a tertiary hospital from 2014 to 2018." *PLoS One*, 2019.

### 2.10 Species-specific dynamic models

Specific models for Staphylococcus aureus.

- **Techitnutsarut, S. & Chamchod, F.** "A mathematical model for antibiotic resistance in Staphylococcus aureus populations." *Math. Biosci.*, 2021.

### Gap noted

The paper does not yet cite a within-host **metabolic** model. This is the natural quantitative complement to the antimetabolic-solution proposal (see the Metabolic page) and is a possible methodology contribution for future drafts.

## 3. SARIMA Model

### Intuition — what the letters mean

Before the formal notation, the plain-language idea. The name is built up one layer at a time:

| Letter | Stands for | What it adds |
|--------|-----------|--------------|
| **AR** | AutoRegressive | Today's value depends on the **past values** of the series itself. |
| **I**  | Integrated (differenced) | Works with the **year-over-year change** instead of the level, to remove the trend and stabilise the series. |
| **MA** | Moving Average | Corrects using the **errors** of past forecasts. |
| **S**  | Seasonal | Captures **cycles that repeat** (every 12 months, every 4 quarters…). |
| **X**  | eXogenous | The key of SARIMAX: lets you feed in **external variables** that help explain the series. |

In one sentence: **SARIMA** forecasts a series using *only its own past*; **SARIMAX** adds the "X" — **outside variables** that also influence what you are predicting.

### SARIMA vs SARIMAX — a worked example

Suppose you want to **forecast monthly *Klebsiella pneumoniae* resistance to carbapenems** in a hospital.

**With SARIMA (no X):**

> "I estimate this month's resistance by looking at the resistance of previous months, its rising trend, and the seasonal pattern (it climbs in winter, when admissions rise)."

It uses only the resistance history. Nothing else.

**With SARIMAX (adding the X):**

> "I use all of the above **plus the hospital's carbapenem consumption** (an external variable). If a lot of carbapenems were prescribed this month, I know the **selection pressure** pushes resistance upward in the following months."

Here antibiotic consumption is the **exogenous variable (X)**. It is not resistance itself, but it *influences* it — and adding it improves the forecast. Other typical exogenous inputs in this context: ICU bed occupancy, temperature/season, infection-control policies, reported outbreaks.

**The underlying distinction:**

- **Endogenous** — what you want to predict (resistance). The model explains it from its own past.
- **Exogenous (X)** — something external that *pushes* the endogenous variable but that you are not modelling; you only use it as a "clue".

### A note on this platform

The Time Series page is implemented with the `SARIMAX` class (Section 3, Specification below), but it **passes no exogenous regressors** — so in practice it runs as a **pure SARIMA** (see Limitation 10). This is deliberate. The long-horizon projections (to 2040) are a single series anchored to published sources; adding exogenous variables would require **also projecting those variables into the future** (how much carbapenem will be consumed in 2038?), which would inject uncertainty with no hard data to anchor it. Staying with SARIMA keeps the model simpler, more transparent, and consistent with the project's **determinism** rule — no assumptions about future covariates we cannot anchor.

### Specification

The Time Series page fits a **SARIMAX(1,1,1)(1,1,0,12)** model to monthly synthetic resistance data.

Full notation: SARIMAX(p,d,q)(P,D,Q,s).

### Parameter Breakdown

| Parameter | Value | Meaning |
|-----------|-------|---------|
| `p = 1`  | Non-seasonal AR order | Current value depends linearly on the previous month's value. |
| `d = 1`  | Non-seasonal differencing | First-order differencing to remove the trend. |
| `q = 1`  | Non-seasonal MA order | Current value depends on the previous month's forecast error. |
| `P = 1`  | Seasonal AR order | This year's January depends on last year's January (period 12). |
| `D = 1`  | Seasonal differencing | Year-over-year differencing. |
| `Q = 0`  | Seasonal MA order | None. |
| `s = 12` | Seasonal period | 12 months. |

### Mathematical Form

After differencing:

```
(1 - phi_1·B)(1 - Phi_1·B^12)(1 - B)(1 - B^12) y_t = (1 + theta_1·B) ε_t
```

with `B` the backshift operator and `ε_t ~ N(0, σ²)` white noise.

### Fitting

Fitted via `statsmodels.tsa.statespace.sarimax.SARIMAX` with `enforce_stationarity=False`, `enforce_invertibility=False`, `maxiter=200`. MLE through Kalman-filter likelihood evaluation.

### Forecasting

`results.get_forecast(steps=horizon)` produces:

- **Point forecast** (`predicted_mean`).
- **95% confidence interval** (`conf_int(alpha=0.05)`) — grows with horizon as cumulative forecast variance accumulates.

### Fallback

If statsmodels is not available or convergence fails, the code falls back to linear extrapolation:

```python
coeffs = np.polyfit(t, series.values, 1)
forecast = np.polyval(coeffs, t_future)
CI = forecast ± 1.96 · std(residuals)
```

The fallback is indicated in the model label on the chart.

### Diagnostics

- **AIC / BIC** — model-fit information criteria.
- **Residual std** — typical magnitude of one-step-ahead errors.
- **Residual plot** — visual inspection for remaining structure.
- **ACF / PACF correlograms** — autocorrelation structure for model-order validation.

## 4. Intervention Scenario

### Approach

The intervention scenario models a hypothetical 30 % reduction in the resistance trend, representing the combined effect of stewardship, infection control, and new drug introductions.

### Implementation

```python
bau_mean = forecast.predicted_mean.values
last_val = series.iloc[-1]
deltas = bau_mean - last_val
intervention_mean = last_val + deltas * 0.7
```

Applied to the magnitude of change rather than the direction. Both scenarios share the same starting point (last observed value).

### Interpretation

The 30 % figure is illustrative, not evidence-based. It demonstrates the principle that coordinated interventions can alter trajectories — not the exact impact of any specific policy.

## 6. Confidence Intervals

### SARIMA Forecast CI

Computed analytically from the model's forecast error variance:

```
CI = forecast_mean ± z_{0.025} · sqrt(cumulative_forecast_variance)
```

Symmetric, monotonically widening with horizon.

### ACF / PACF Significance Bounds

Standard Bartlett bounds:

```
± 1.96 / sqrt(N)
```

with `N` the series length.

### Super-exponential ±3 yr Envelope

The ±3-year band on the resistance curve is a **temporal shift**, not a statistical CI — see Section 1.

## 7. Limitations and Assumptions

### General

1. **Conceptual index, not a measurement.** The Resistance Pressure Index is a constructed composite for visualisation. It cannot be directly validated against any single observable.
2. **Aggregation masks heterogeneity.** A global index of 70 could mean 90 in South Asia and 40 in Scandinavia.
3. **Synthetic time-series data.** The monthly data on the Time Series page is synthetic; calibrated to plausible trends but not a real surveillance feed.

### Super-exponential Curve

4. **Closed-form, not fitted.** The parameters (K, A, r, b) are calibrated by hand to anchor points published in the literature. They are not the output of a likelihood-maximising fit, so no goodness-of-fit metric applies.
5. **Single-species shorthand.** A super-exponential model abstracts away the per-species mechanisms (KPC plasmid spread, ESBL emergence, MRSA decline). Use the Pathogens and Time Series pages for species-resolved views.
6. **Saturation assumption.** The model still asymptotes to K = 100. Real resistance dynamics could in principle reverse (as MRSA has in Europe). The paper's argument is that, *within the classical-antibiotic paradigm*, no such reversal is currently in sight.

### SARIMA

7. **Stationarity after differencing.** If the underlying trend is super-exponential rather than linear-in-differences, SARIMA may underestimate future resistance.
8. **Gaussian errors.** Resistance series can be skewed and outlier-prone (outbreaks).
9. **Constant parameters.** Model parameters are estimated from the full series and assumed constant.
10. **No exogenous variables.** No antibiotic-consumption, policy, or new-drug-approval covariates.

### Intervention Scenario

11. **Linear proportional reduction.** 30 % applied uniformly across the horizon. Real interventions have delayed onset and variable effectiveness.
12. **No feedback effects.** The intervention scenario does not model behavioural response (reduced investment if resistance appears to be declining).

### Carbapenem Spotlight

13. **Hardcoded anchors.** Values are illustrative, anchored to two published sources (Murray 2022 baseline + Tai 2025 qualitative trajectory). Exact numbers per pathogen split should not be cited as-is — refer to the source publications for the underlying figures.
